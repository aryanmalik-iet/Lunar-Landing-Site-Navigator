Dashed tracks: Sun over the next 15 days, Earth over the next 30 days (dots every 24 h). Flat horizon assumed — local hills and crater walls are not modelled, so real sunset/Earthset can come earlier, especially near the poles.

🎓 The Moon always keeps the same face toward Earth, so from the near side Earth hangs almost still in the sky — it only wobbles a few degrees (called libration). From the far side Earth is never visible, so landers there need a relay satellite (China's Queqiao-2 did this for Chang'e-6). The Sun, on the other hand, crawls across the sky so slowly that sunrise to sunset takes about two Earth weeks.

    🎓 How the Moon helps people on Earth every day: The Moon's gravity pulls Earth's oceans into two tidal bulges, creating two high tides and two low tides every ~24 hours 50 minutes. When the Moon is closest to Earth (perigee, ~356,500 km) and lined up with the Sun (New or Full Moon), "King Tides" can flood low-lying coastal roads and mangroves. Predicting these cycles protects fishermen, coastal families, marine ecosystems, and disaster-response teams.

    🎓 Landers usually touch down shortly after local sunrise: the low Sun casts long shadows that help cameras spot boulders and craters, and the spacecraft then gets the whole ~two-week day of solar power. The 12-month view shows "lunar seasons" — the Moon's axis is tilted only 1.5°, so seasons are tiny, but at the poles that small tilt decides whether a crater rim is lit or dark.
